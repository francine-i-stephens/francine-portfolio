The Extra‑Tap Advantage

A pre‑production A/B/C experiment for Workday’s mobile Day‑One onboarding experience. The design that required one extra tap cut the time for an initial step in the onboarding process by more than half, overturned the team’s hypothesis, and surfaced a deeper information‑architecture risk before rollout to every HCM customer.

At a Glance

2× Faster median time on task 24 sec (Variant C) vs. 52 sec (Variant A)

4× Fewer Taps to complete 3 taps vs. 12 taps

+20 pts Higher Findability 80% vs. 60% (Variant B)

+23 pts Higher Usability 93/100 vs. 70/100 (Variant A)

Problem

Workday’s mobile onboarding experience was being redesigned to let frontline and deskless workers complete Day‑One setup entirely within the app. The team needed to place a new manager‑personalized message into an already dense information architecture. The options were to locate it either on the main landing page for immediate visibility or inside a dedicated personalized tab that required an extra tap. The question: Would the extra tap hurt performance?

Role & Context

I led study design, prototype testing, data analysis, and reporting. The cross‑functional team included a PM, researcher/designer, and design technologists. Prior research showed manager‑authored content mattered especially to frontline workers, but the team needed evidence for exactly where that content should live.

Hypothesis & Design

The team expected Variant B, placing the message under Announcements on the landing page, to win. Instead, Variant C (inside a separate “For You” tab) won outright.

  • Variant A — Highlights: message in a highlight reel at the top of the landing page.
  • Variant B — Announcements: message as the first card under Announcements.
  • Variant C — For You: message inside a dedicated personalized tab.

3000 frontline workers were randomized into three groups. Success was defined as tapping the correct message, regardless of path.

Process & Decisions

  • Success counted the moment a user tapped the correct element, not how many taps it took.
  • A warm‑up task was included only to measure exploration behavior, but later revealed deeper IA issues.
  • Distractor content was added to mimic real customer apps.
  • Exploration behavior was statistically adjusted to isolate design effects.

Results

The assumption that didn’t hold

Variant C, the design with an extra tap, won on every metric that mattered. It wasn’t just a placement decision; it corrected the team’s mental model of how frontline workers navigate.

The one‑tap paradox

Variant C required one more tap to enter the tab, yet it delivered:

  • Fastest median time on task: 24 sec (vs. 33 sec for B, 52 sec for A)
  • Fewest taps: 3 (vs. 7 and 12)
  • Highest perceived findability: 80%
  • Highest perceived usability: 93/100
  • Highest user confidence: 91%
  • Success rate: tied for highest at 96%

77% of Variant C users reached the message in exactly two taps once inside the tab — the theoretical minimum.

An IA Risk Ahead of Full Rollout

The warm‑up task surfaced a deeper information‑architecture problem: users gravitated toward the “For You” tab even when it had nothing to do with the task. This wasn’t just a placement insight; it was an architecture signal.

Before replacing the legacy Onboarding Dashboard for all HCM customers, the underlying navigation model needed to be addressed.

Recommendations

  • Adopt Variant C (“For You” tab) for manager welcome messages.
  • Treat the IA‑overload finding as a rollout risk, not a footnote.
  • Reduce reliance on Plan‑page shortcuts for personal content.
  • Extend the “meet users where they already look” principle to other Day‑One content.

Impact

Variant C was pushed to production. The IA‑overload finding triggered additional work on learning content and navigation friction, shaping the broader onboarding experience beyond this single experiment.

Learnings

Minimizing taps is a common design heuristic, but the winning design had the most steps. What mattered was alignment with user mental models. The warm‑up task, originally intended as throwaway data, revealed a critical insight.
